your call
What it is
SkyView is a map of courses. Anything drawn on it has to hang on a course identity. A CPL row can only do that through the receiving college course — the course the college awards the credit against. Most CPL rows do not name one.

And the chain narrows again on the way to the screen: of those exhibits, 2,647 resolve to a course identity in the committed crosswalk, covering 2,319 identities — of which 1,490 are on the map SkyView ships. So a CPL layer keyed on the map today can reach about a quarter of the exhibits MAP holds, and the limit is the missing receiving course, not the map.

What I propose
Build the CPL face as a second lens on the same points, not a second map — and have it say its own coverage out loud. A point lights when an exhibit reaches it; the panel leads with the credential, the agency and what it earns instead of the course title; and a line at the top of the view reads “1,490 of 6,388 exhibits reach a course on this map.” The other three quarters are not drawn, because there is nowhere honest to draw them.
Why
A CPL view that quietly shows a quarter of the record looks like the record. Naming the fraction on the surface costs one line and is the difference between a lens and a claim. It also puts the real work in view: the missing receiving course is a MAP data-quality item, not a rendering one.

A residual quirk: the grouping key can be a non-word

small, reversible
What it is
The key that groups spellings is stemmed, and a few stems are not English: news stories keys as new story, series solutions as sery solution, mechanics’ lien as mechanic lien.
Measured today
The key is never shown — the card displays a spelling a college actually published, so no reader sees sery solution. It matters in exactly one case: if one course card carried both senses of such a word, the two would be grouped. That case does not occur in the corpus today.
What I propose
Leave it. Record the class in the lane file so a future session reading sery solution in a debug dump knows it is a key and not a bug, and revisit only if a real collision turns up.
Why
Fixing an invisible key costs a special case in the stemmer and buys nothing a reader can see. Naming it costs two lines and stops the next session from “fixing” it into a regression.

6

Does a reviewer’s added skill carry their name?

your call
What it is
A reviewer can type a skill the catalogs do not name. Today it shows as added by a reviewer with the day it was staged, and no person attached — because the page has no signed-in identity.
The precedent already in the register
DR-19 governs the shared memory table and answers the same question: sessions propose only; a row lands proposed and becomes trusted through a corroboration gate. DR-17 governs the credit-recommendation vocabulary and keys curator judgment so a rebuild can never overwrite it. Your own standing rule is stronger than either: a curator’s knowledge is a first-class input, attributed, never laundered into an anonymous value.
What I propose
Yes — name and date, from the reviewer phrase, on every added skill, and show it on the card the way the medoid description names the college that wrote it. An added skill lands proposed; it displays immediately to the person who added it and to curators, and joins the published outline only once a second curator or you marks it verified — the DR-19 shape, unchanged.
Why
An unattributed skill is indistinguishable from an imputed one, which is exactly the confusion this layer was built to remove. And the outline is heading for faculty readers: the question they will ask about a skill no catalog names is who says so.

7

Does removing a skill need a second person?

your call
What it is
Removal is the asymmetric one. Adding a skill puts something on screen that a reader can weigh; removing one takes evidence away, and the imputation will not put it back on its own.
Measured today
A removal is already recorded, not inferred — the struck name is listed under Removed by a reviewer with a way to put it back, so nothing vanishes silently. What is missing is only who may make it stick.
What I propose
No second person for the staging, yes for the publish. One reviewer may strike a skill and it disappears from their view at once, marked and reversible. It leaves the published outline only on a second curator’s agreement — the same gate as an addition, in the other direction. A removal nobody seconds stays visible with the reviewer’s objection recorded beside it.
Why
Symmetry with item 6 keeps one rule instead of two, and it matches the register’s principle: we route to determinations rather than making them. A disputed skill carrying a named objection is more useful to a faculty reader than a skill that quietly is not there.

Which register row governs a curate write — and who owns it?

your call
What it is
Rule 10 a3 says a new write surface routes through Governance before it ships. The skills layer would be the first thing SkyView ever writes, and it does not fit an existing row cleanly: DR-04 governs course identity, DR-07 credential titles, DR-17 the wording of credit. A skill statement is a fourth vocabulary.
Measured today
Every owner in the register is null — that is the register’s own open question OQ-01. A new row would be the twenty-somethingth decision right with no accountable person, which is the condition the register exists to make visible rather than to repeat quietly.
What I propose
A new row, DR-22 — “What a course is said to teach”, covering the skills layer and anything later added to the outline of record, maintained in a new table behind the reviewer phrase, with the DR-19 propose-and-verify gate from items 6 and 7. And you as its named owner, which would make it the first row in the register with one.
Why
Folding it into DR-04 would put skill statements under the identity layer, whose rules re-lock the moment M-IDs are declared faculty-published — two different lifecycles under one right. A separate row keeps the curate phrase’s scope decision (still open) from being answered by accident. Naming an owner is a small act here and the register has been waiting for a first one.
