# The same arithmetic can read as withholding or as investment

> **One-sentence summary** — When you show an audience how a pot of money is
> divided, the notation and the grouping make an argument on their own; get them
> wrong and a page of entirely true figures reads as an accusation against the
> people who wrote it.

## Context

The CPL implementation funding explainer had to explain how a one-time $35M
appropriation becomes a dollar figure per college, for CO colleagues and possibly
CA Finance staff. Every figure on the page was generated by the funding tab's own
engine and none was wrong. Sam still read it back as: *"a CEO at a college might
question why so much funding is being withheld by the CO."* Full story:
[`docs/cpl_funding_lessons.md`](../cpl_funding_lessons.md) (2026-08-22).

## The claim

**Three separable choices decide whether an allocation reads as withholding or as
investment. None of them is the numbers.**

### 1. The instrument

A **waterfall** exists to reconcile an account, so every non-terminal line is a
deduction. Point one at money that is *meant to be spent* and it argues that
spending is loss — the red minus on "Chancellor's Office staff" says a college
lost that money, when what it says in fact is that two people are employed to
support colleges. Recoloring does not fix it, because the subtraction is the
diagram's grammar, not its palette. **Match the instrument to the argument: a
waterfall for reconciliation, a set of destinations for allocation.** Retiring
the waterfall here removed the last red on the page as a side effect — it was the
only consumer.

### 2. The words

Name the **beneficiary** of each amount, not the fact that it stopped short of
someone. *"Held by the Chancellor's Office"* and *"statewide CPL projects and
technology — the work all 115 colleges draw on"* are the same $9,759,692.
Likewise a section heading: *"What is set aside before anything reaches a
college"* → *"Where the appropriation goes."*

### 3. The grouping

Which amounts share a box is an argument about who the money is for. Showing a
$1,000,000 noncredit set-aside as its own box, beside the college pool, reads as
money being taken out of the colleges' share. Folding both into one
**$25,240,308 allocated to the 115 colleges and the four noncredit campuses**
reads as the whole effort being funded. Identical inputs.

### The corollary: a negative framing is rarely load-bearing

Before deleting a blunt sentence, check where its content *also* appears. *"That
figure is a ceiling, not a check… Show none of it, earn none of it"* was replaced
with *"what a college receives is driven by its own CPL results, as they
happen"* — and nothing was lost, because the qualifying step already said "earns
nothing against it" and the earning step already said "half its target, half that
pot." **Warmth cost no information.** The reverse also happens: a genuinely
reassuring fact can be buried inside a warning. *"It earns nothing against it,
and what it would have earned is held in reserve"* contains *a college's
allocation is not redistributed while it works toward the baseline* — the half
the reader actually wants, hidden behind the half they don't.

## Two things that constrain the rewrite

⚠️ **The arithmetic must still close, visibly.** A Finance reader subtracts. When
the boxes were $35,000,000 and $9,759,692 and $24,240,308, the difference was
$1,000,000 and the page did not say where it went. Every destination must be on
the page, and the residual figure should be **defined** as the total minus the
others in code, so no two boxes can drift apart on a rebuild.

⚠️ **A friendlier grouping must not state something false.** "Also refer to the
four noncredit campuses whenever you say 115 colleges" is right about the
*allocation* and wrong about the *credit-FTES split*, which those campuses are
correctly not part of. Apply the reframing where it is true and leave the rest;
then say plainly where the excluded group is handled, so its absence from a list
titled "Every college" is answered rather than noticed.

## How we got here

Five rounds on one page (PRs #1285–#1289), each one a reader's reaction rather
than a defect: the tone note, the crimson note, the grouping note. The pattern
only became visible because the same audience read the same true numbers three
times and kept arriving at the wrong conclusion.

## When this does NOT apply

Internal reconciliation, an audit trail, or a variance report — there the
subtraction *is* the subject and a waterfall is the right instrument. This note is
about documents whose job is to persuade a recipient that a program is investing
in them.
