How this funding model works

Most of the one-time appropriation is allocated directly to the 115 colleges, in proportion to how much teaching each one does. The rest funds a dedicated amount for the four noncredit campuses, the statewide CPL projects and technology every college draws on, and two Chancellor’s Office posts supporting that work. No college is offered less than $150,000 over the two years.

What a college receives is driven by its own CPL results, as they happen. The figure allocated to a college is what is available to it across the two years; what it draws down tracks the prior-learning credit it actually puts on students’ records, measured from that college’s own MAP data against three priorities. A college qualifies by doing three straightforward things, and from there the funding follows the outcomes.

The average sits well above the typical college because a handful of very large colleges pull it up. Most colleges are offered less than the average, which is worth saying out loud before anyone reads $210,785 as what a college can expect.

Step one

How much each college is offered

A college’s size is measured in credit FTES — the Chancellor’s Office’s usual measure of how much teaching a college does. Add up all 115 and you get 1,069,182. A college that does 2% of the state’s teaching starts with 2% of the pot.

Two things then adjust that starting figure:

  • A minimum of $150,000 over the two years. Fifty colleges’ proportional share came out below that, so they are lifted to it — funded from within the same pot, which is why the larger colleges’ shares come down a little.
  • A guaranteed $76,923 for each of the 13 rural colleges. It counts toward their minimum first, so it is the rural money rather than the main pot that carries a small rural college most of the way to $150,000.

Step two

What a college has to do to qualify

Three things, none of them onerous, and all of them checkable from MAP:

  1. A CPL Coordinator or Counselor listed in MAP, so a student’s request reaches a real person.
  2. A participation request by 1 November 2026. A college’s vice president or chief executive can submit it from the college’s own row on the funding page.
  3. At least 75% of enrolled veterans’ Joint Services Transcripts uploaded to MAP.

⚠ Qualifying does not change the size of the offer. A college that has not yet met the baseline has the same amount allocated to it, and that allocation is not redistributed while it gets there — what a college has not yet earned stays in reserve for it rather than being shared out to the colleges that moved first.

Step three

How the money is earned

The offer is split across three priorities. Each one has a share of the money and a single thing it measures. The order and the shares below are the ones currently set in the model.

Every priority is measured in the same currency: units of prior learning, converted to FTES at the college’s own calendar — 30 semester units make one FTES, or 45 on a quarter calendar.

Turning a pot into a target

Each pot is divided by a price to give the target. The price is the 2026-27 state rate of $5,649.63 per FTES, multiplied by that priority’s funding factor. All three factors are currently set to 1.0, so every priority is paid at the plain state rate and the three targets differ only because the shares do.

What the funding factor is for. Raising it above 1.0 pays more per FTES, so that pot is earned with fewer units — a premium on work you want to encourage. Lowering it does the reverse. It is a separate dial from the share: the share decides how many dollars sit behind a priority, the factor decides how hard they are to earn.

A college that shows half its target on a priority earns half that pot. Showing more than the target does not pay more — the pot is the ceiling.

⚠ One consequence worth knowing. Because the $150,000 minimum is paid for from inside the same pot, a college above the minimum earns about $5,060 for each FTES of prior learning it shows rather than the full $5,650, while a small college lifted to the minimum reaches its target on far less. That is the floor doing exactly what it was put there to do, but it does mean the rate a college earns at is not identical everywhere.

Step four

When the money arrives

Two timing choices are switched on in the current model, and neither changes how much a college can earn.

  • The whole two-year amount is offered in year one. Colleges need to build capacity at the start — staff time, faculty review of credit recommendations, local process work — so waiting until year two for half of it would work against the point. Anything unspent rolls forward. Because all the money is on the table in year one, the targets shown are the full two-year figures.
  • Year two mirrors year one. The two years are deliberately identical, so editing one keeps the other in step rather than letting them quietly drift apart. Nothing is overwritten: switching the mirror off leaves both years exactly as they were.
