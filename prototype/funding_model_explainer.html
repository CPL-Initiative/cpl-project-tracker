How this funding model works

Most of the one-time appropriation is allocated directly to institutions — to all 118 institutions — the 115 colleges plus the three noncredit-only campuses — in proportion to how much teaching each one does, credit and noncredit together. The rest funds the statewide CPL projects and technology every college draws on, and two Chancellor’s Office posts supporting that work. No institution is offered less than $150,000 over the two years, and none more than $400,000.

What an institution receives is driven by its own CPL results, as they happen. The figure allocated to a college is what is available to it across the two years; what it draws down tracks the prior-learning credit it actually puts on students’ records, measured from that college’s own MAP data against three priorities. A college qualifies by doing three straightforward things, and from there the funding follows the outcomes.

The average sits well above the typical college because a handful of very large institutions raise it. Most institutions are allocated less than the average, so the median is the better guide to what an institution can expect.

Step one

How much each college is offered

An institution’s size is measured in full-time equivalent students, the Chancellor’s Office’s standard measure of instructional volume, counting credit and noncredit instruction together. An institution accounting for two percent of the statewide total begins with two percent of the amount allocated to institutions.

Two adjustments are then applied to that proportional figure:

  • A minimum of $150,000 over the two years. Forty-five institutions’ proportional share fell below that amount and is brought up to it, funded from within the same total.
  • A maximum of $400,000 over the two years. Six institutions’ proportional share exceeded that amount and is held there. The difference, $262,241, is redistributed across the institutions below the maximum. An institution held at the maximum is assessed against a correspondingly smaller target, so it earns at the same rate as every other institution above the minimum.

Step two

What a college has to do to qualify

Three requirements, each verifiable from MAP:

  1. A CPL Coordinator or Counselor listed in MAP, so a student’s request reaches a real person.
  2. A participation request by 1 November 2026. A college’s vice president or chief executive can submit it from the college’s own row on the funding page.
  3. At least 75% of enrolled veterans’ Joint Services Transcripts uploaded to MAP.

⚠ Qualifying does not change the size of the offer. A college that has not yet met the baseline has the same amount allocated to it, and that allocation is not redistributed while it gets there — what a college has not yet earned stays in reserve for it rather than being shared out to the colleges that moved first.

Step three

How the funding is earned

The offer is split across three priorities. Each one has a share of the money and a single thing it measures. The order and the shares below are the ones currently set in the model.

Every priority is measured in the same currency: units of prior learning, converted to FTES at the college’s own calendar — 30 semester units make one FTES, or 45 on a quarter calendar.

How a share becomes a target

Each priority’s share is divided by a price to produce its target. The price is the 2026-27 state rate of $5,649.63 per FTES, multiplied by that priority’s funding factor. All three factors are currently set to 1.0, so every priority is paid at the plain state rate and the three targets differ only because the shares do.

What the funding factor is for. Raising it above 1.0 pays more per FTES, so that priority’s share is earned with fewer units — a premium on outcomes the Chancellor’s Office intends to encourage. A factor below 1.0 has the opposite effect. It is a separate dial from the share: the share decides how many dollars sit behind a priority, the factor decides how hard they are to earn.

An institution that reaches half of a priority’s target earns half of that priority’s share. Exceeding the target does not increase the amount earned: the share is the ceiling.

⚠ One consequence worth knowing. Because the minimum is paid for from inside the same total, an institution above the minimum earns approximately $5,158 for each FTES of prior learning it shows rather than the full $5,650, while a small college lifted to the minimum reaches its target on far less. That is the floor doing exactly what it was put there to do, but it does mean the rate a college earns at is not identical everywhere. The maximum does not add a second discount: a college held to it is asked for proportionally less prior learning, so it earns at the same rate as every other college above the minimum.

Step four

When the funding is disbursed

Two timing choices are switched on in the current model, and neither changes how much a college can earn.

  • The whole two-year amount is offered in year one. Colleges need to build capacity at the start — staff time, faculty review of credit recommendations, local process work — so waiting until year two for half of it would work against the point. Anything unspent rolls forward. Because all the money is on the table in year one, the targets shown are the full two-year figures.
  • Year two mirrors year one. The two years are deliberately identical, so editing one keeps the other in step rather than letting them quietly drift apart. Nothing is overwritten: switching the mirror off leaves both years exactly as they were.
