Catalog Course Description
This course examines the central role mass media plays in daily life. Starting from a microsociological standpoint, students will examine how knowledge and experiences are increasingly mediated by the mass media in its various forms. The course also explores the effect of media, including television, radio, newspapers, and the Internet, on social institutions which in turn permeate and shape public policy, the economy, education, and even the family. The course will examine ways in which mass media contributes to social/cultural power and stratification and will use the “process of mutual determination” to examine the relationship between media, individuals, and society. This course may be offered in a distance education format.

Objectives
Upon successful completion of this course, the student will be able to:

Discuss the significance of mass media in a contemporary, democratic society and how this compares globally.

Analyze the role that media plays in shaping and organizing public discourse.

Describe and compare the key theoretical perspectives in the study of mass media.

Compare and contrast the role of media in a cross cultural context.

Critically analyze cultural consumption of media including the construction of meaning and sub cultural resistance.

Explain what is meant by "media power" and the relationship of this term to issues of ownership and control.

Analyze the role mass media plays in the creation, persistence, and disruption of social inequality.

Student Learning Outcomes
Broader than Objectives, SLOs are often comprised of what the student can do as a result of combining several course objectives.

Theoretical Approaches:  Compare the various theoretical approaches to the study of mass media.

Key Role of Mass Media:  Describe the key role the mass media play in shaping and organizing public discourse, social stratification, and social structure.

Societal Effects of Mass Media:  Compare the individual versus societal effects of mass media.

Mass Media & Social Institutions:  Evaluate the nature of the relationship between mass media and social institutions including the economy and government.

Structure/Organization:  Explain the structure and organization of mass media.

Audience:  Describe the role of “the audience” as consumers of mass media.

Outside-of-Class Assignments
Typical Assignment

Essay Assignment: Media Analysis Project

This assignment allows students to explore the relationship between structure and agency as it relates to media and society. Through research and analysis, students will analyze how this debate plays out in relation to a specific topic of interest. Ultimately, this project is designed to strengthen student’s understanding of media and society than readings and lecture alone would provide.
Picking Your Topic
The text focuses on three areas in which this structure/agency debate exists:

Relationships Between Institutions
Relationship Within Institutions
Relationships Between Institutions and the Public

The first thing in choosing a topic is to decide which of these three areas is of interest. Doing so is the first step in narrowing down the list of possible topics. From here, start to narrow the focus of the question. The more specific the question the better the paper is likely to be. Here are some examples of questions that may work…

Short Essay Exam Questions:

Using the “Star Wars” activity in class, explain the difference between encoded and decoded meaning. How are these concepts related to passive versus active consumption?
Why has the media become the component around which political campaigns organize themselves? Your answer should incorporate evidence from the film, Wag the Dog, and the Reagan, Bush, and Gore campaigns discussed in the text.
What is “interpretative resistance”? Are the soap opera fans discussed in the Scardaville article practicing this?

Sociology of Mass Media promotes equity through course objectives, content, assessment, and pedagogical practice. Its promotion of critical thinking through the focus on scientifically responsible data collection and analysis is a key component of inclusive and equitable pedagogy. Course content is committed to the inclusion of academic research, analysis, and reflections from diverse scholars in the field with the intention of centering structurally marginalized voices in the study of statistics in the behavioral sciences. Assessments in the course are designed to recognize and integrate multiple ways of knowing and to value the lived experiences of the diverse student population we serve. Classroom policies and procedures are subject to continual equity evaluations by students to guard against unintentional barriers to student success
