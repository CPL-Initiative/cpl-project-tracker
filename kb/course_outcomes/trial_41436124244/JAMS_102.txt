Catalog Description
Explores alternative story forms by combining text, still photographs, video clips, audio, graphics and interactivity to tell stories in the most compelling and informative way. Focuses on using a variety of media to tell different parts of a story for presentation in digital and online platforms.

Student Learning Outcomes
At the conclusion of this course, the student should be able to:
Report and construct news stories using a variety of media.
Identify and apply the legal and ethical responsibilities of the reporter.
Apply digital storytelling strategies across contemporary media platforms.
Course Objectives
At the conclusion of this course, the student should be able to:
Construct multimedia news stories through blog, website and social media posts
Produce feature and news stories using audio and video
Edit audio and video to tell a variety of story forms
Assess digital storytelling strategies – Knowing when and how to use traditional print, audio, video, multimedia, other visual and social media
Develop digital research strategies for multimedia packaging and storytelling
Course Content
Course Lecture Content

I. Lecture.  SELECTING THE RIGHT MEDIUM FOR WRITING AND REPORTING WITH CLARITY AND COHERENCE 

Examine the characteristics of strong, clear prose.
Choosing and exploiting the appropriate medium.
Writing and selecting key details for clarity and coherence.
Explore the fundamental models of multimedia storytelling.
Writing scripts for multimedia stories.
Writing scripts for video, photography and audio to create slideshows.
Matching media - video, photos, audio, graphics/maps and text - to different kinds of stories.
Understand how combining specific media leads to clarity in writing and reporting a story.  

 

II. Lecture.  TELLING RESPONSIBLE STORIES

Explore the value choices multimedia storytellers make in creating messages.
Recognize how ethical lapses occur when storytellers fail to reason through messages.
Understand the damaging effects of stereotypical language, image, sound and video.
Apply and practice careful and thoughtful editing to avoid stereotyping.
Understand ethical multimedia storytelling techniques without compromising a story.
Research commercial databases, Internet sources, government documents and public records to verify and validate material sources.
Investigate sources for reliability.  
Utilizing checklists for accuracy.

 

III.   Lecture. WRITING FOR SPECIFIC AUDIENCES.

Develop text, still photographs, video clips, audio, graphics and interactivity for audience.
Examine how audiences seek information about a specific event or story.
Identify how audiences are characterized, measured and targeted.
Apply audio writing techniques to improve the listening experience.
Utilize digital and online multimedia storytelling to bridge audiences from various mediums.
Apply reportorial techniques for covering political, social, and life issues in online and digital stories. 

 

IV.  Lecture.  NONLINEAR STORYTELLING.

Understand that telling nonlinear stories means being flexible in choosing media forms.
Recognize how a structured single narrative impedes navigation through a story.
Utilize a variety of decision-making tools for selecting media formats.
Understand how to divide a story into its logical, nonlinear parts.
Explain the importance of history in telling the story.
Recognize how to organize stories in sections rather than chronological order.
Evaluate best practices to edit and report complex stories.

 

V.  Lecture.  TELLING THE STORY AND DEVELOPING STRUCTURE.

Examine and model alternative story forms.
Experiment with openings of multimedia stories, beyond the Inverted Pyramid format.
Develop narrative techniques.
Practice and apply alternative structures.
Recognize specific audience strategies in writing and structuring a story.
Examine word choices, specific details and key elements to advance a story.
Understand how to transition from the theme paragraph to nut graphs, to foreshadowing, to conflict.
Examine scene, place and environment techniques.  

 

VI.   Lecture.  WRITING THE BODY AND ENDING OF A MULTIMEDIA STORY.

Understand that the body of the story is elemental.
Recognize why simple sentence constructions are better than complex sentences for pacing and comprehension
Understand that strong active verbs are critical to compelling storytelling from scene to scene.
Practice and apply movement, time and sentence patterning techniques to advance a story.
Understand that mixing facts, data and examples with narration moves the storyline, and supports a multimedia story’s main theme.
Analyze how to conclude and wrap a multimedia story.
Apply and practice storytelling techniques like tiebacks, anecdotes, dialogue, scenes and quotes to conclude the story.

What learning activities or assignments are required outside of class?
Reading Assignments
Students will critically read essays and treatises relevant to the course’s objectives. 
Analyze various media (Internet, TV, radio, films, speeches, public presentations, type, logotype, podcasts, slideshows and art) for impact, narrative style, and cultural and social context. 
Students will be encouraged to look beyond a text to observe examples of multimedia structures, and persuasion in new media/multimedia communications. 
Students will analyze and appreciate works of philosophical and cultural importance related to the field of multimedia reporting.                     

Typical reading assignment examples:

1. Read an article about how multimedia journalism began and its burgeoning influence on global media.

2. Examine NASA’s webcast of the Mars Rover Landing noting how they combine still pictures, graphics, statistics and complex figures into an entertaining and informational multimedia package.

3. Read a mid 20th century magazine and compare its content and visual messages to a contemporary multimedia website, looking particularly for ways to analyze, evaluate and interpret how a multimedia story differs in how it is told.

Writing Assignments

Writing:

Prepare explanatory and persuasive essays addressing critical positions in multimedia storytelling and reporting. 
Students will demonstrate the ability to analyze issues and apply logical reasoning in evaluating topics related to the study of multimedia reporting. 
Assignments will range from short, concise responses to more extensive and sustained analyses.

Typical writing performance examples:

1. Analyze a political website and note its construction and storytelling methods  In a two to three page essay explore how stories are written and packaged and assess how that packaging and structure might impact and influence audiences.

2. Monitor a breaking news source for a day. You may use any national or local news website, or an agency such as AP, Tribune Media or Reuters. Look for stories that are breaking, and see how they develop during the day. If you are part of a group, divide the day into blocks, or shifts. Take an hour or two each to monitor the news. Cover several sources – two or more agencies, or two or more news outlets – and keep a log of what story breaks, when, and how it develops. See who is first with this news and how fast rivals catch up with it. Remember to use terms that you have learned related multimedia and New Media storytelling.

Other Assignments

Students will produce independently reported works of multimedia storytelling throughout the semester to demonstrate proficiency in course objectives. 

NOTE: Multimedia stories and exam assignments must account for 75 percent of the student’s final grade for the semester. Final multimedia stories and longer documented story for online or digital presentation will total at least 10 minutes of content. All multimedia scripts must be typewritten and follow AP Style and MLA format.

Standards of Achievement
How will learning be assessed? (Multiple measures must be used)

The analysis and interpretation of multimedia storytelling require a variety of critical thinking skills. Students must:

1. Identify and respond to the storyteller’s central purpose;

2. Determine the philosophical/artistic assumptions implicit in the content and structure of the work;

3. Apply recognized criteria of media writing and storytelling excellence;

4. Value his/her own life experience as a tool for understanding non-linear stories;

5. Distinguish between his/her own responses to a work and the work itself;

6. Identify levels and shades of meaning in language, image, sound and video;

7. Recognize foreshadowing, vivid imagery, dialogue and value of conflict in storytelling;

8. Value the close relationship or identity of form and content in structuring multimedia stories.
