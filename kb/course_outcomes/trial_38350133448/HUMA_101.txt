CATALOG COURSE DESCRIPTION:
This interdisciplinary course is designed for students interested in meeting general education requirements in humanities. The course develops students' understanding and appreciation of humankind's cultural heritage from the Upper Paleolithic (ca. 40,000 BCE) to approximately 1400 CE.  A survey is made of the literature, philosophy, music, painting, architecture, and sculpture of both Western and non-Western civilizations.

STUDENT LEARNING OBJECTIVES:
Upon successful completion of the course the student will be able to:
Upon successful completion of the course the student will be able to:
Characterize some of the world civilizations from Upper Paleolithic to approximately 1400 CE.
Assess the influence of geographical, social, and historical factors on these civilizations.
Interpret, analyze and criticize disciplines in the arts, literature and philosophy of these cultures.
Interpret, analyze and criticize the origins, forms and content of some of the arts, literature and philosophy of the various time periods and cultures studied.
Section II
1. COURSE OUTLINE AND SCOPE:
A. Course Content:
The following topics are included in the framework of the course but are not intended as limits on content. The order of presentation and relative emphasis will vary with each instructor.
Course Lecture Content
The description and characteristics of world civilizations and development of cultures from Upper Paleolithic to approximately 1400 CE, including but not limited to the cultures of Africa, Asia, Europe, and the Americas:
Upper Paleolithic
Neolithic
Ancient Middle East (Mesopotamia, Egypt), South Asia (India), East Asia (China, Japan, Southeast Asia), and the Americas
Classical Europe (Greece and Rome)
Medieval Europe, Middle East (Mesopotamia, Egypt), Near East (India), Far East (China, Japan, Southeast Asia), and the Americas
Religious variety from global polythesim to Western monotheism
Influence of developmental factors on civilization:
Geography
History
Economics
Social Structure (including issues of gender and gender preference)
Technology
Cross-cultural influences
Interdisciplinary perspective of cultural disciplines, including but not limited to interpretation, analysis, and criticism of the Arts:
Painting (e.g., cave art, on pottery, on architecture, etc.)
Sculpture (e.g., monumental, relief, freestanding, architectural, etc.)
Architecture (e.g., megalithic, monumental, ritual/religious, etc.)
Miscellaeous art forms (e.g., calligraphy, mosaic, reliquary, etc.)
Music (e.g., Greek tonal, Asian traditions, medieval forms, etc.)
Literature (e.g., oral traditions, poetry, drama, essays, etc.)
Interdisciplinary perspective of origins, forms, and content of some of the art, literature and philosophy of the various periods and cultures studied:
Religion (including but not limited to primal sacred traditions, mythologies, Native American traditions, Judaism, Christianity, Islam, Confucianism, Taoism, Hinduism, Buddhism, etc.)
Philosophies (including but not limited to ancient Greek, including the materialists, sophists, Socrates, Plato and Aristotle, etc.)
Art and Architecture (including but not limited to religious influences on temples, artistic expression, etc.)
Connections between world cultures and their art, literature, and philosophies/religions (including but not limited to examples of syncretism, etc.)
B. Reading Assignments:
Reading assignments are required and may include, but are not limited to, the following:
Assigned textbooks in the humanities.
Primary sources (e.g., the Epic of Gilgamesh, the Vedas, etc.).
Essays on the humanities from scholarly journals.
Internet reports on humanities topics.
C. Writing Assignments:
Writing assignments are required and may include, but are not limited to, the following:
Book reviews critiquing topics in the arts and literature.
Short essays comparing and contrasting philosophies and religion.
Critiques of current periodicals concerning humankind's cultural heritage.
Research papers on topics in the humanities.
D. Appropriate Outside Assignments:
Outside assignments may include, but are not limited to, the following:
Reading and writing assignments as specified in the course syllabus.
Library, electronic and other archival research on topics in the humanities.
Viewing of assigned/recommended media materials on the arts.
Observations (e.g., field trips, attendance at pertinent lectures and/or conferences on philosophy/literature).
Analytical semester projects on comparative world religions.
Research papers on topics in the humanities.
Critiques of current periodicals concerning humankind's cultural heritage.
Critiques of live cultural performances (e.g., the oral tradition, morality plays, Greek music, etc.)
E. Appropriate Assignments that Demonstrate Critical Thinking:
Critical thinking assignments are required and may include, but are not limited to, the following:
Analyze and summarize primary and secondary sources of ancient literature.
Compare and contrast various elements of non-Western and Western religions.
Differentiate amongst artistic movements.
