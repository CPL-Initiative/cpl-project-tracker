How this funding model works

Most of the one-time appropriation is allocated directly to institutions — to all 118 institutions — the 115 colleges plus the three noncredit-only campuses — in proportion to how much teaching each one does, credit and noncredit together. The rest funds the statewide CPL projects and technology every college draws on, and two Chancellor’s Office posts supporting that work. No institution is allocated less than $150,000 across the two years, and none more than $400,000.

What an institution receives is driven by its own CPL results, as they happen. The figure allocated to a college is what is available to it across the two years; what it draws down tracks the prior-learning credit it actually puts on students’ records, measured from that college’s own MAP data against three priorities. An institution establishes eligibility by meeting three baseline requirements; from that point the funding follows the outcomes.

Step one

How much each institution is allocated

An institution’s size is measured in full-time equivalent students, the Chancellor’s Office’s standard measure of instructional volume, counting credit and noncredit instruction together. Across the 118 institutions in the model the combined total is 1,151,171 FTES. An institution accounting for two percent of that total begins with two percent of the amount allocated to institutions.

Two adjustments are then applied to that proportional figure:

  • A minimum of $150,000 across the two years. Forty-five institutions’ proportional share fell below that amount and is brought up to it, funded from within the same total.
  • A maximum of $400,000 across the two years. Six institutions’ proportional share exceeded that amount and is held there. The difference, $262,241, is redistributed across the institutions below the maximum. An institution held at the maximum is assessed against a correspondingly smaller target, so it earns at the same rate as every other institution above the minimum.

How noncredit funding is calculated

Noncredit funding is calculated from the noncredit FTES each institution reports to MIS, and is earned against the priorities set out in this model. Noncredit FTES are counted where the instruction originates in the noncredit program and the credit is awarded at a credit college in the district.

    Step two

    What a college has to do to qualify

    Three requirements, each verifiable from MAP:

    1. A CPL Coordinator or Counselor listed in MAP, so a student’s request reaches a real person.
    2. A participation request by 1 November 2026. A college’s vice president or chief executive can submit it from the college’s own row on the funding page.
    3. At least 75% of enrolled veterans’ Joint Services Transcripts uploaded to MAP.

    Meeting the baseline does not change the size of the allocation. An institution that has not yet met the requirements is allocated the same amount, and that allocation is not redistributed in the interim. Funding an institution has not yet earned is held in reserve for it rather than reallocated to institutions that qualified earlier.

    Step three

    How the funding is earned

    Each institution’s allocation is divided across three priorities. Each priority carries a share of the allocation and a single measure. The order and the shares below are those currently set in the model.

    Every priority is measured in the same currency: units of prior learning, converted to FTES at the college’s own calendar — 30 semester units make one FTES, or 45 on a quarter calendar.

    How a share becomes a target

    Each priority’s share is divided by a price to produce its target. The price is the 2026-27 state rate of $5,649.63 per FTES, multiplied by that priority’s funding factor. The factors currently set in the model are 1.0, 1.0 and 1.0.

    What the funding factor does. A factor above 1.0 pays more per FTES, so the priority’s share is earned with fewer units of prior learning — a premium on outcomes the Chancellor’s Office intends to encourage. A factor below 1.0 has the opposite effect. The factor is a separate setting from the share: the share determines how much funding stands behind a priority, and the factor determines the volume of prior learning required to earn it.

    An institution that reaches half of a priority’s target earns half of that priority’s share. Exceeding the target does not increase the amount earned: the share is the ceiling.

    One consequence of the minimum. Because the minimum is funded from within the same total, an institution above the minimum earns approximately $5,158 for each FTES of prior learning it records rather than the full $5,650, while an institution brought up to the minimum reaches its target on considerably less. This is the intended effect of the minimum, and the consequence is that the effective rate is not uniform across institutions. The maximum introduces no second reduction: an institution held at the maximum is assessed against a proportionally smaller target, so it earns at the same rate as every other institution above the minimum.

    Step four

    When the funding is disbursed

    Two timing settings are in effect in the current model. Neither changes the amount an institution can earn.

    • The full two-year amount is made available in year one. The capacity this work requires — staff time, faculty review of credit recommendations, and local process design — is concentrated at the outset, and withholding half of the allocation until year two would delay it. Funding not drawn down in year one carries forward. Because the entire allocation is available in year one, the targets shown are the full two-year figures.
    • Year two mirrors year one. The two years are held identical so that a change to one is reflected in the other. Nothing is overwritten: disabling the mirror leaves both years as they were.

    What is a choice, and what is a given

    Some of the figures in this model are policy decisions the Chancellor’s Office can change. Others are given, and the model takes them as it finds them.
